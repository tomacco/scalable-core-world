Field notes of a perpetual tinkerer

Old clocks & swords

Two crafts, one obsession: things forged to outlast their makers, and machines patient enough to keep time. This is where I keep what I have learned taking such things apart and — usually — putting them back together.

IAbout

The person at the bench

Around here I answer to coresmith. By trade and by temperament I am a tinkerer: I collect, repair, and now and then honestly ruin two kinds of old made things — mechanical clocks and edged steel. They look like opposites. One measures time; the other, for centuries, decided how much of it you had left. But both are the same lesson in different metal.

What I love is the moment a mechanism stops being a mystery. A clock that has been silent for decades, a blade dark with neglect — you take it down to its parts, learn why each one is shaped exactly the way it is, and the whole thing turns out to be reasonable. Nobody added a piece for decoration. Every curve is load-bearing.

I keep no relics behind glass. A clock should tick and a blade should flex, or they are just sculpture. So the things on these pages are working things — kept honest, kept oiled, and handed on a little better understood than I found them.

IIHorology

Clocks, or the mechanics of patience

The heart of any mechanical clock is the escapement — the small, stubborn device that lets the gear train advance one measured step at a time instead of unwinding all at once. Everything else is just storage and gearing. The escapement is where energy is rationed into seconds, and it is the part that talks back to you: the tick is the anchor catching, the tock is it letting go.

Power comes from a wound mainspring or a hanging weight, patiently bled through the going train to the escape wheel. A pendulum or balance sets the beat, and here is the quiet miracle: within small swings, a pendulum keeps very nearly the same period whether it swings wide or narrow. That near-independence is what lets a heap of brass and steel argue with the rotation of the Earth and mostly win.

Escapement
The gate that rations power into beats. A good deadbeat gives back nothing — the second hand steps and stays.
Going train
The gears between power and escapement. Their ratios are why one turn of the mainspring becomes twelve hours on the dial.
Beat
Put it in beat and the tick and tock fall evenly. Out of beat, a clock limps and stops. You tune it by ear.
Patina
Blued screws, honey-warm brass, a dial gone the colour of old paper. Wear is a record, not a wound — I clean, I do not erase.
IIIThe blades

Swords, or geometry with an edge

A blade is applied geometry. Long before it is a weapon it is a set of decisions about steel: how the thickness falls away from hilt to point (the distal taper), where the mass sits, and how the cross-section trades a keen edge against a spine that will not fold. Hold a good one and it feels alive in the hand — the balance point lands where your control is, not out at the tip dragging your wrist.

The romance is in the metallurgy. Differential hardening leaves a hard, keen edge over a softer, springy back, so a blade can take a cutting edge and still bend and return rather than shatter. Etch and polish an old one and the boundary shows as a hamon, a frozen tideline where the steel changed its mind. And the groove down the flat is a fuller — it lightens and stiffens the blade, the way an I-beam does. It was never for blood.

Distal taper
The blade thinning from hilt to point. Get it right and the thing balances; get it wrong and it is a crowbar.
Fuller
The channel along the flat — structural, not gory. Less mass, more stiffness, same reach.
Hamon
The visible line of a differential hardening. Part function, part signature of the smith who quenched it.
Return
A sound blade flexes and springs back true. If it takes a set and stays bent, the heat treatment failed it.
